Why Process Supervision Survives Wrong Traces

Private reading notes by Aayushman

September 12, 2026 · updated September 27, 2026 · password protected · 25 min read

Count the letter a in the word banana, modulo 3, starting from 1. You can do it with a pencil, and a tiny Transformer can do it exactly, in two different ways. One way writes down the running count. The other writes only the final answer. Train that Transformer on correct final answers and it stays near chance. Train it on the running count and it solves the problem. Train it on traces that are mostly wrong, and the clean traces still carry the lesson. This note is a walkthrough of why.

Do the count by hand

Start at 1. The word is b, a, n, a, n, a. A letter that is not a leaves the count alone. A letter that is a adds one, and we wrap around at 3, so the count lives in \(\{0, 1, 2\}\).

b is a miss, so you stay at 1. a is a hit, so you go to 2. n stays at 2. a wraps to 0. n stays at 0. The last a brings you back to 1. The answer is 1. The running count was \(1, 2, 2, 0, 0, 1\).

Nothing about that arithmetic is hard. What is hard, once the word is long, is getting a learning signal out of the final number alone. The serious version of the same task is a 16-letter word, still modulo 3. One two-block Transformer can compute that answer exactly, whether you ask it for the answer alone or for the running count first. Trained on 20,000 correct final answers for 32,000 updates, it stays at 30–50%. Chance is 33%. Trained on the running counts, it reaches 100% within 12,000 updates. Trained on traces that are wrong at every step 60% of the time, each of them still ending at the right answer, it reaches 66–95%. The clean 40% of those traces, trained by themselves, reach 100%.

So the correct answers were a weak teacher. The clean steps were a strong one. The wrong steps did not point the model at a different rule. They taxed the clean ones.

Two programs, one small Transformer

The gap is not that the answer-only solution is too big to fit. You can write both solutions down by hand, as weights of the same architecture: two blocks, three attention heads, no LayerNorm. Both programs are exact on every counting prompt with up to 700 letters. On the held-out words used in the experiments, the hand-written weights score 100%.

Think of the residual stream as a row of named slots. The embedding writes the letter, or the value, and the position. Everything else starts at zero. Both programs share the first block. That block copies the query letter onto each letter of the word, then marks a match: 1 if the letter equals the query, 0 otherwise. On banana with query a, the marks are \(0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1\).

The second block is where the two programs split.

Chain-of-thought does one addition per generated number. At each step a head copies the previous value, another head copies this letter’s match bit, and a small MLP adds them modulo 3. A hit shifts the one-hot value by one. A miss leaves it alone. Then the model emits that new value and moves on.

The answer-only program does not emit those values. At the colon it averages every match bit, scaled so the average is the total count, copies the starting value, and decodes “start plus count.” All six additions happen at one position. It needs no extra depth. It needs more hidden units: \(3K(n+1)\) instead of \(2K\), where \(K\) is the number of states and \(n\) is the length of the word.

If you only look at capacity, both of these are solved. The hand-written chain-of-thought weights and the hand-written answer-only weights both continue the prompt correctly. Training does not. From the same random start, chain-of-thought reaches 100% at every length we tried, within 16,000 updates. Outcome supervision on a long word stays near chance.

Outcome supervision learns it. Counting modulo \(K\) does not, once the word is long. The architecture can represent the answer. The answer is a bad place to put the credit.

Why the final number is a weak teacher

A fresh network does not contain a feature for “the parity of the whole word.” Almost everything it can express at initialization looks at no letters, or at one letter. On the two-block Transformer, 99.6–99.8% of the Jacobian energy at initialization sits at order 0, and nearly all of the rest at order 1. Order \(r\) means “this feature combines \(r\) of the moves.”

The final count is the opposite kind of object. After many letters it depends on many of them. A loss on that count can only reward a feature where the feature’s order matches the answer’s order. If the network has simple features and the answer is a high-order function, the overlap is small, and the gradient is small.

That statement is not special to Transformers. Take any differentiable model, and any task of the form \(s_t = F(s_{t-1}, x_t)\) with answer \(y = h(s_n)\). Split the model’s Jacobian and the centered answer by which moves they depend on, the Efron–Stein decomposition. Pieces that depend on different sets of moves are uncorrelated, so a feature of order \(r\) is paid only by the order-\(r\) piece of the answer. Write \(A_r\) for how much of the answer lives at order \(r\), and \(W_r\) for how much of the model lives there. At uniform prediction,

\[ \|\nabla L_{\text{answer}}\| \le \sum_r \sqrt{A_r W_r}. \]

The right-hand side is the whole theorem in one line. Credit is the overlap of two spectra. \(A_r\) is a fact about the task. \(W_r\) is a fact about the model you have right now. You can attain the bound: a one-parameter model whose Jacobian is exactly one piece of the answer saturates it.

Proof: only matching orders get paid

The gradient of \(-\log p_y\) in the answer logits is \(p - e_y\). At uniform prediction \(p = u = \mathbf{1}/K\),

\[ \nabla L_{\text{answer}} = -\mathbb{E}\bigl[J^\top (e_y - u)\bigr]. \]

Condition on the prompt. The moves are independent, so both the Jacobian \(J\) and the centered answer \(e_y - u\) split into pieces \(J_S\) and \(a_S\) indexed by subsets \(S\) of the moves, and pieces on different subsets are uncorrelated. Only matching subsets survive, and grouping them by size \(r = |S|\) gives one credit vector \(g_r\) per order. Cauchy–Schwarz inside a subset, then again across the subsets of size \(r\), bounds \(\|g_r\|\) by \(\sqrt{A_r W_r}\). The triangle inequality over \(r\) bounds the full gradient.

Away from uniform prediction the same decomposition still holds for the part of the gradient that sees the label. An extra term depends on the model’s own prediction and does not see \(y\). Uniform prediction is the moment when the label-dependent part is the whole gradient.

On some tasks the answer spectrum itself shrinks as the word gets longer. Call the task reversible when every move is a permutation of the state: counting is, because adding 0 or 1 modulo \(K\) just rotates the states. Average a random move and differences between start states get blurred. The blurring factor is \(\lambda\), the largest amount a mean-zero vector can shrink in one averaged move. \(\lambda = 0\) means one move erases the start. \(\lambda = 1\) means something about the state is never forgotten. For counting a letter that appears with probability \(p = 1/A\),

\[ \lambda = \bigl|1 - p + p\, e^{2\pi i / K}\bigr|. \]

With a four-letter alphabet this is \(1/2\) for a coin-flip count (modulo 2) and about \(0.66\) for modulo 3. An order-\(r\) feature then receives answer signal at most on the order of \(\lambda^{n-r}\). Each letter the feature does not look at multiplies the credit by \(\lambda\). For modulo 2, a feature that ignores the word is damped by \(\lambda^n = 2^{-n}\). Eight letters already means a factor of \(1/256\). The exponent is tight: there is an order-\(r\) counter whose gradient really is \(\Theta(\lambda^{n-r})\).

Proof: why each unseen letter multiplies by \(\lambda\)

Write the answer as the start state pushed through the product of the move matrices. Split each move into its average plus a fluctuation, and expand the product. The term that fluctuates on a set \(S\) and averages everywhere else is exactly the piece of the answer that depends on \(S\). An averaged move shrinks mean-zero vectors by at most \(\lambda\). A fluctuation does not increase the mean square, because it is a centered permutation. Collecting one \(\lambda^2\) for every position outside \(S\) gives

\[ \mathbb{E}\|a_S\|^2 \le \lambda^{2(n-|S|)}\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{K}\Bigr). \]

Plug that into the overlap bound and the factor \(\lambda^{n-r}\) falls out. For counting, the averaged move is a circulant matrix, diagonal in the Fourier basis of the cyclic group, and \(\lambda\) is the largest nontrivial eigenvalue.

You can see the rate on the real network, if you sum the gradient over every prompt. That is only practical for a small alphabet, so the measurement uses modulo 2 with four letters, where \(\lambda = 1/2\), rather than the modulo-3 story above.

The answer signal dies at the predicted rate. The step signal does not. Two-block Transformer at initialization, modulo 2, four letters. Gradients are summed over every prompt. A fit on the answer column gives \(0.508\) per letter. The other two seeds give \(0.513\) and \(0.480\).

From 2 letters to 10, the answer gradient falls about 240-fold. Half, eight times, is 256, so the measurement is sitting on the prediction. The step gradient rises by a factor of about 2.4 over the same range. It has nothing to forget: a step target looks at one transition.

In training this shows up as a plateau. The answer-only run can leave chance only by building high-order features, and it has almost no gradient with which to build them. When a run does leave, about half of its new Jacobian energy is at orders 2 and up. That is the network finally growing the features the answer was willing to pay for.

\(\lambda\) is not the whole story. A Boolean circuit of 8 gates and a small register machine both mix slowly (\(\lambda = 0.82\) and \(0.95\)), so the exponential is mild. But 98.9% and 99.9% of their answer spectra sit at orders 2 and up. The overlap is small because the spectra do not line up, not because \(\lambda\) is tiny. Outcome supervision stays near chance on both. Clean step traces reach 82% and 99.9%.

Put the target on one step

A step target depends on one transition: the displayed previous state, and the current letter. That is an order-1 feature, which is the kind of feature a fresh network already has. Nothing has to travel the length of the word.

You can dial this. Show the state every \(k\) steps, and let the model predict from the last shown state. Each target is now a count of \(k\) letters, so the same damping applies with \(n\) replaced by \(k\). Full chain-of-thought is \(k = 1\). Asking only for the answer is \(k = n\).

On modulo 2, eight letters, after 8,000 updates: showing every step gives 72–96%. Showing every second step gives 49–61%. Showing every fourth step, or only the answer, sits at chance. One extra unshown letter already spends most of the lesson, at this budget.

Along the way, the chain-of-thought network is not a pure one-step machine. If you replace the displayed states by random ones, the local rule for the last state really does depend on a single letter. At update 6,000, almost all of the network’s letter energy is still at orders 2 and up. Once the run has solved the task, that share falls to about a third. The training data is consistent with a local rule and with a rule that counts across the word, and the network uses a global route before it settles on the local one.

Wrong steps tax the lesson

Now suppose some of the traces are wrong. Not a typo in one place: after every displayed state, the trace shows a wrong successor, and the final answer is still correct. This is harsher than ordinary label noise, because a wrong state is also the input to every later prediction. It is tempting to think the errors will teach a different rule.

They do not, as long as the errors are scattered and the model cannot tell a clean trace from a corrupted one. The reason is local. To predict the next state you need the displayed previous state and the current move. On a reversible task that pair has the same distribution on clean traces and on corrupted ones. A model that only looks at that pair is blind to which kind of trace it is reading.

While it is blind, a scattered wrong step is an exactly reversed share of a right step. If there are \(K\) possible states, the wrong successor is uniform on the \(K-1\) states that are not the true one, and the label gradient of that wrong step equals \(-1/(K-1)\) times the label gradient of the right step. Mix a fraction \(\rho\) of clean traces with the rest corrupted and the state credit is the clean credit times a margin,

\[ \mu(\rho) = \frac{K\rho - 1}{K - 1}. \]

The margin is positive exactly when more than \(1/K\) of the traces are clean. It does not depend on how long the word is. For modulo 3, the sign changes at \(\rho = 1/3\): a corpus that is 30% clean has margin \(-0.05\), and a corpus that is 40% clean has margin \(+0.10\).

Here is the modulo-3 arithmetic, so you can see the minus sign rather than take it on faith. Suppose the true next state is 0. The centered one-hot is \(\bigl(\tfrac{2}{3}, -\tfrac{1}{3}, -\tfrac{1}{3}\bigr)\). A scattered error picks state 1 or state 2 with equal probability, and the centered target of that mixture is \(\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{6}, \tfrac{1}{6}\bigr)\). That is exactly half of the true target, pointing backwards. Half is \(1/(K-1)\).

Proof: a scattered error is a reversed share

Scatter puts mass \(1/(K-1)\) on every state other than the true successor \(F\), so its distribution is \(R_F = (\mathbf{1} - e_F)/(K-1)\). Subtract the uniform vector \(u = \mathbf{1}/K\):

\[ R_F - u = \frac{\mathbf{1} - e_F}{K-1} - \frac{\mathbf{1}}{K} = -\frac{e_F - u}{K-1}. \]

The identity is algebraic. It holds at every parameter, before any assumption about the network. If the Jacobian and the true successor have the same joint law on clean contexts and on corrupted contexts, the state gradient on corrupted traces is \(-1/(K-1)\) times the clean state gradient. Mix them with weight \(\rho\) and the coefficient of the clean gradient simplifies to \(\mu(\rho)\). The answer token is untouched, because every trace, clean or not, ends at the correct answer. If the two context laws differ by total variation at most \(\delta\), the gradient identity holds up to an error \(2\delta\) times the largest Jacobian.

On the trained network the measurement is very close to this. At modulo 3, eight letters, three seeds, the corrupted-trace gradient has cosine \(-0.999\) with the clean one, and its norm is \(0.498\) to \(0.501\) times the clean norm. The prediction was \(-1\) and \(1/2\). The net gradient flips direction between \(\rho = 0.3\) and \(\rho = 0.4\), which is where the margin changes sign.

Clean steps carry the credit. Wrong steps only shrink it. Count modulo 3 over 16 letters, scattered errors, 32,000 updates, three seeds. Chance is 33%. Exact-trace accuracy is what the step loss is aimed at. The clean subset of each corpus, trained alone, is essentially perfect.

That is the margin just turning positive, plus the fact that every corrupted trace still ends at the right answer, so the answer token keeps a bit of signal of its own. On the exact trace, which is what the step gradient is trying to get right, the same corpus is ahead on two seeds and within a point on the third.

The clean subset is the sharper comparison. The mixed corpus receives \(\mu(\rho)\) times the clean gradient, and \(|\mu(\rho)|\) is less than 1 whenever any traces are wrong. Training on the clean traces alone should do better than training on the mixture. It does, at every row: those clean subsets sit at 100%.

Two warnings, both measured. First, if the answer is easy, wrong traces do not beat it. At eight letters, modulo 3, \(\lambda^8\) is only about \(0.04\), and correct answers alone reach 100%, 67.7%, and 99.9% by 32,000 updates. No mixed corpus beats that. Second, one coherent wrong rule is different from scatter. If every error is the same permutation of the truth, the corrupted gradient is the gradient of that competing rule. What the two rules share is learned at full strength. What distinguishes them is learned at weight \(2\rho - 1\), so you need the corpus to be more than half clean. Shifting every count by \(+1\) is a rule of that kind, and it commutes with adding a match bit, so the answer is still readable: \(y\) is the displayed final state minus the length. At 32,000 updates the answer stays at least 95% accurate for every \(\rho \le 0.4\), while the exact trace stays at most 5%.

A single slip is different again. Slip once, then compute correctly from the wrong state, and every later transition is a genuine local step. On modulo 3, eight letters, a late slip in 70% of traces leaves step accuracy at 90, 90, 59%, against a clean run’s 100, 68, 64%. An early slip at the same rate drops it to 21–31%. Scatter at every step, at that short budget, needs the corpus to be about 99% clean before exact-trace accuracy hits 50%. The late slip behaves like the theory. The early slip does not, at a horizon where two of the three clean seeds have not yet left the plateau.

What the margin does once you train

There is a toy model where you can see the answer plateau exactly. Let the model be a transition table, one row-stochastic matrix per move, shared across positions. At the uniform table, multiplying \(n\) centered perturbations together leaves only the term in which every factor is perturbed. Every derivative of the answer loss of order less than \(n\) vanishes. A small alignment \(\delta\) with the true rule escapes only after time on the order of \(K^n \delta^{2-n}\). The step loss, at the same point, has gradient \((K-1)/K\). The answer is an \(n\)th-order flat point. A single step is not.

Proof: the uniform table is flat for the answer

Let \(U\) be the uniform matrix and let \(E\) be centered, meaning it kills the all-ones vector from both sides. Then \(UE = EU = 0\). In a product of terms \(U + E_t\), every summand that contains both a \(U\) and a perturbation vanishes. The product equals \(U\) plus the product of all \(n\) perturbations. The answer probability is \(1/K\) plus a polynomial of degree \(n\), so its derivatives of order \(1, \ldots, n-1\) vanish at the uniform table, and so do those of \(-\log\) of that probability.

Along the true rule, give every row logit \(\alpha\) on the correct successor and 0 elsewhere. The answer loss collapses to a function of one variable, \(\log K - \log\bigl(1 + (K-1)\beta(\alpha)^n\bigr)\), and the first nonzero derivative at \(\alpha = 0\) has order \(n\). The step loss is a single factor and has derivative \((K-1)/K\) there.

While the network cannot tell the traces apart, scattered corruption is clean training with the labels smoothed by \(\mu(\rho)\). Under plain gradient flow the corrupted run is the clean run on a slower clock. A budget of \(B\) updates succeeds when the clean learning time fits inside \(\mu(\rho)\, B\). On modulo 3 with eight letters, that predicts reliability thresholds of \(0.54\) for scatter and \(0.66\) for a coherent shift, at 32,000 steps. The measured thresholds are \(0.37\) and \(0.52\), lower than the prediction. The network learns from the messy corpus faster than the slowed clock allows. That is the direction you expect from AdamW, which mostly ignores a uniform rescaling of the gradient. On the Boolean circuits the same prediction lands on the other side of the measurement, so treat the slowed clock as an estimate of the threshold, not a bound. Plain SGD at the same learning rate did not leave the plateau within 16,000 steps even on clean traces.

Blindness ends once the rule is partly known. A correct first step is then evidence that the trace is clean, and a reader that uses that evidence continues exactly. On modulo 3, eight letters, a linear probe for “is this trace clean?” is at chance on the first displayed state and between \(0.80\) and \(0.99\) on the second. Flip the first displayed state of a trained network and it follows the local rule on what it was shown 98.8% of the time. It follows the original gold continuation 1.2% of the time. After a solved modulo-2 run, the last step really is local: scramble the earlier states or the earlier letters and accuracy on the final state stays at 99–100%. Scramble the displayed previous state and it falls to chance.

The same split, off of counting

The Boolean task applies 8 random NOT, CNOT, SWAP, or Toffoli gates to 4 bits. The register machine applies 16 random invertible instructions to two registers in \(\mathbb{Z}_{17}\) and reports one of them. Both are reversible, both forget slowly, and both have answers whose mass sits at high order. Outcome supervision scores 7.7% on the circuits, against a chance of 6.25%, and 6.3% on the register machine, against a chance of 5.9%. Clean traces reach 82% and 99.9%. Extra tokens that do not carry the intermediate state do not close the gap. On the circuits the outcome score falls with depth, from 88% at 2 gates to 7.8% at 8, while step supervision stays near 90%. In the trained network the step gradient stays aligned with an oracle local-transition gradient, cosine about \(0.8\). The answer gradient does not, cosine about \(0\).

Wrong steps tax this credit here too. Traces that are 30% clean reach 67.7% on the register machine, against 6.3% for correct answers. On the circuits the full corpus beats correct answers once 60% of the traces are clean. Concentrate the same amount of error on one coherent wrong rule and register accuracy at 30% clean falls from 69.8% to 13.3%. After a correct first step the network continues exactly 90.6% of the time.

A pretrained model shows the same split. Rank-8 LoRA on SmolLM2-135M, trained on the 8-gate circuits for 800 updates, reaches \(89.7 \pm 4.9\%\) under clean step supervision and \(9.9 \pm 0.2\%\) under outcome supervision. Chance is 6.25%. At half the traces clean, scattered, the answer is \(28.2 \pm 0.8\%\). Exact rollouts under clean supervision land within a point of the answer accuracy, and the per-step accuracy \(0.987\) compounds as \(0.987^8 \approx 0.90\), so the mistakes look roughly independent.

A fitted successor table recovers the rule as soon as the margin is positive, that is, from \(\rho = 1/K\). A trained Transformer needs more reliability than that. Four or five times more data barely moves the circuit threshold, from \(0.84\) down to \(0.77\). Depth moves it a lot, from \(0.84\) down to \(0.27\) between 2 and 6 blocks, because depth roughly halves the number of updates the clean run needs. More data does not.

What to keep, and where this stops

A correct answer is a correct target for the whole computation. It pays a feature only where that feature overlaps the answer. On a long reversible computation the overlap is small, either because the task forgets its start or because the answer lives at orders the network does not yet have. A step target is local, so it pays the one-step feature directly. When the bad steps are scattered, each of them contributes a fixed negative share of a good step. They shrink the lesson. They do not replace it.

A few things are deliberately not claimed. The overlap identity holds for any sequential task. The closed form with \(\lambda\) needs reversible moves. The wrong-trace argument needs them too, because that is what keeps the displayed state uniform. The source of the supervised credit is exact. The path a nonlinear optimizer takes from there is measured, not proved. How blind a trained network is, is measured by the probe, not proved. The slowed-clock prediction for the reliability threshold lands below the measurement on counting and above it on the circuits, so it is an estimate. When the damping is mild, correct answers do teach the task, and wrong traces do not beat them. The tasks are synthetic. Whether the same mechanism is what makes step supervision matter for a large reasoning model is open.

The object here is supervised imitation of traces you were given. It is not a process reward model, and it is not a claim that a wrong final answer is useful. In this corruption the wrong steps contain no valid transition. They never help on their own. Whatever the mixture learns, it learns from the clean steps, at a discount.
